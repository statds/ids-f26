1  Midterm Project: PM2.5 and Congestion Pricing

New York City began charging most vehicles entering the Congestion Relief Zone (CRZ) on January 5, 2025. The CRZ covers Manhattan’s local streets and avenues south of and including 60th Street, with exemptions for the FDR Drive, West Side Highway/Route 9A, and connections from the Hugh L. Carey Tunnel to West Street (Metropolitan Transportation Authority 2024). Did the odds of a high-PM2.5 day at CRZ monitors change after the policy began, accounting for regional background conditions? We define a high day relative to the pre-policy CRZ data; this is a study-specific threshold, not a health standard.

This project builds on a published analysis of congestion pricing and air quality (Fraser et al. 2025) and extends its data through August 2026. Fraser et al. analyze continuous daily PM2.5 using prediction-based counterfactuals; the high-day outcome and segmented logistic model here are a separate course analysis, not the paper’s model. The analysis can show whether measurements changed around the policy date. It cannot, by itself, show that the toll caused a change. Weather, wildfire smoke, traffic, monitor operations, and other events can affect PM2.5 at the same time. Your report must separate the model’s estimated association from a causal conclusion.

Use the same virtual environment of the ids-book project to ensure reproducibility of your report on my computer.

You may use AI tools to brainstorm, explain data-science concepts and workflows, consult programming documentation, and suggest or debug code. You are responsible for running, checking, and understanding AI-suggested code; conducting the analysis; and writing your own interpretations and conclusions. Do not ask an AI tool to complete an exercise or produce your report. Disclose AI assistance using the statement in the report template.

Two ZIP files are provided, covering June 1, 2023, through August 31, 2026: NYCCAS data and AirNow data. Each ZIP contains one hourly CSV file. Keep both ZIPs in a local data/ folder next to your report. You may read the CSV files directly from the ZIPs or extract them first. Do not include the data files in your submission.

  • nyccas.csv contains readings from six NYCCAS monitors inside the CRZ, with location and site information.
  • airnow.csv contains readings from four regional AirNow monitors used to estimate background PM2.5: Flemington, Rutgers University, Chester, and Paterson.

NYCCAS records are hourly averages, in micrograms per cubic meter, and the ObservationTimeUTC field explicitly identifies UTC. AirNow’s datetime field has no timezone offset in the CSV, but the AirNow hourly data specification reports observation times in GMT (UTC). In both files, a timestamp marks the beginning of its hour. The two files use different column names and formats. Read the source descriptions and inspect the files before deciding how to clean and combine them. AirNow may contain special or invalid concentration codes; determine how to handle them from the values and documentation. Do not treat a missing monitor reading as zero.

The data come from the NYC Community Air Survey (NYCCAS) archive (New York City Department of Health and Mental Hygiene 2026) and the AirNow API (United States Environmental Protection Agency 2026a). The AirNow hourly data specification documents its GMT timestamp convention (United States Environmental Protection Agency 2026b). NYCCAS readings are preliminary and its historical files can be revised. The accompanying data README explains which source snapshots contribute to each period and documents NYCCAS correction changes and records removed after sensor problems. Cite both data sources in your report.

Exercises

1.1 Inspect and clean the hourly source files.

  1. State the observational unit in each file and identify the field that distinguishes monitors.
  2. Identify each file’s timestamp and concentration fields, state the time zone and units, and explain what time an hourly timestamp represents.
  3. Standardize the needed column names and parse timestamps as UTC. Report null-cell and duplicate monitor-hour counts. For each monitor, report the absent-hour count within its first-to-last observation window. Identify the monitor-month with the most absent hours, and distinguish absent hours from null cells and time before a monitor began reporting.
  4. Inspect unusual AirNow values, including negative codes. Identify the invalid code or codes, report how many rows they affect, and explain the exclusion rule. Apply the rule to a working copy and leave the source files unchanged.
  5. After excluding invalid codes, apply the paper’s strict range, \(0 < PM_{2.5} < 25.5\) µg/m³. For each source, report eligible, retained, and excluded row counts. Also report how many valid zero and high readings the rule removes, and explain why some high readings could be real events.

1.2 Create daily data and merge the sources.

  1. Create one monitor-day row for each NYCCAS and AirNow monitor with an observation that day. Calculate each row’s daily maximum and report the number of monitor-day rows for each source.
  2. Average the available AirNow monitor maxima for each UTC date. Record the number of contributing monitors and report a frequency table of dates by number of contributing monitors.
  3. Create dates from the UTC timestamps. Show one example hour whose New York local calendar date differs from its UTC date, and explain why the chosen date convention matters for a daily maximum.
  4. Left-join the date-level background estimates to the NYCCAS monitor-day table. Report row counts before and after the join, validate the expected many-to-one join, and explain how dates without background measurements are represented. Do not join the hourly files directly or replace missing measurements with zero.
  5. Create an indicator that switches on January 5, 2025, and an elapsed-days variable that is zero before and on that date. Retain January 2025 and report the indicator and elapsed-days values for January 4, 5, and 6.

1.3 Explore and visualize the hourly and daily measurements.

  1. Plot CRZ monitor daily maxima with month on the horizontal axis, daily maximum PM2.5 on the vertical axis, and a separate panel for each weekday.
  2. Describe one seasonal pattern and compare two weekdays within a month, referring to the plot. Explain how the facets help make these patterns visible.
  3. For each UTC date, average the available CRZ monitor daily maxima. Plot this series with the daily AirNow background series. Report the mean CRZ-minus-background difference before and after the policy date and the change in that mean difference. Explain why this raw contrast is descriptive.
  4. Show frequency tables of dates by the number of contributing AirNow monitors and by the number of contributing CRZ sites. Explain why missing measurements are not zero-valued measurements.
  5. Plot the daily CRZ-minus-background PM2.5 distributions before and after January 5, 2025. Compare their medians and spreads. Use a two-sided Mann–Whitney U test to compare the distributions: state the hypotheses, report both sample sizes, the test statistic and \(p\)-value, and interpret the result at the 0.05 level. Explain the test’s independence assumption and why seasonality and nearby-day dependence limit its interpretation.

1.4 Model high-PM2.5 days and interpret the results.

For each UTC date, average the available CRZ monitor-specific daily maxima. Define a high day as a CRZ daily mean above its pre-policy 75th percentile. This study-specific cutoff is not a health standard.

Fit a segmented logistic model with time in years from the study start (\(t\)), an indicator for January 5, 2025, onward (\(T_t\)), years since that date set to zero beforehand (\(D_t\)), and daily AirNow background. Fraser et al. use square-rooted background; justify your chosen form. Other covariates, such as month or day type, are your choice. A general model is

\[ \log\left(\frac{P(H_t=1)}{1-P(H_t=1)}\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t + \beta_2 T_t + \beta_3 D_t + g(X_t), \]

where \(H_t\) marks a high day and \(g(X_t)\) contains AirNow background and any other covariates. The coefficients \(\beta_2\) and \(\beta_3\) describe the immediate level change and the change in yearly trend, respectively.

  1. Calculate the 75th percentile of the pre-policy CRZ daily means. State the resulting cutoff and define how it classifies each date as high or non-high.
  2. Make a two-way cross-tabulation of high-day status by policy period (before versus after January 5, 2025). Show counts and percentages within each period. Also report the number of dates and summarize CRZ monitor coverage with a frequency table by number of contributing sites.
  3. Fit the segmented logistic model. Report and interpret the intervention level-change and trend-change odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. Explain your AirNow background term and any other covariates.
  4. Plot observed high days and fitted probabilities over time. Also plot response residuals (observed indicator minus fitted probability) over time. Describe the pre-policy and post-policy patterns, whether the fitted change is an immediate shift, a trend change, or both, and any visible residual pattern.
  5. Conclude by answering the research question. Use the residual pattern, dependence between nearby days, regional background, and possible confounding to explain how far the result supports a causal claim.

Submission. Organize the report around these four steps. The project has 20 subquestions, worth 2 points each, for 40 points total. Include the Python code that produces every reported result, and explain each cleaning and modeling choice.
